Impact — Predatory Finance & Junk Fees

Level B (Impact). Narrative anchors: Act II Rooms 034, 036 (and 039, 037).

What this is

“Junk fees” are low-visibility charges and urgency premiums that stack on top of everyday life. They aren’t just annoying—at scale, they become a quiet extraction system that hits working households hardest.

Act II mechanic: friction is revenue, and urgency is where extraction hides.

Why this matters now

Current path

  • More services shift to fee-based, “premium speed” access.
  • Administrative burden increases time poverty, making urgency common.
  • Households pay extra to avoid penalties, shutoffs, or missed deadlines.

Rebuilt path

  • Reduce or eliminate monetized friction for essentials.
  • Create baseline public options where markets exploit urgency.
  • Measure and publish fee burden like any other cost-of-living indicator.

The reform (plain language)

A junk-fee reform bundle does three things:

  1. Visibility: require clear disclosure and standard reporting.
  2. Limits: cap or ban specific categories (late fees, “processing,” surprise add-ons).
  3. Alternatives: provide a baseline, non-extractive path for essential access.

The enforcement logic is detailed in Mechanics — Junk Fees.

Risks & failure modes

  • Fee re-labeling: charges shift names without reducing burden.
  • Price backfill: firms raise base prices; reform must track net household burden.
  • Access throttling: “free” becomes slow; speed remains monetized.

Metrics we track

  • Household extraction rate: $/household/year in junk fees (modeled + measured).
  • Urgency premium index: extra cost to access “normal speed.”
  • Time recovered proxy: hours/year saved from reduced administrative friction.
  • Regressivity score: burden by income decile.

Projection (what improves and when)

The fastest visible result is household cashflow relief—because fee stacks are immediate. The medium-term gain is time recovered (fewer penalties, fewer loops, fewer “prove it again” steps).
