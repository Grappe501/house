Paperwork

Nothing feels less political than paperwork.

It arrives quietly. Forms, disclosures, acknowledgments, records. Boxes to check. Fields that won’t submit unless they’re filled correctly. Documents that look the same no matter who is holding them.

Paperwork gives decisions a neutral face. Once something is written down, stamped, logged, or filed, it stops feeling like a choice someone made and starts feeling like a fact of the environment.

You don’t argue with paperwork. You comply with it. You adapt your behavior to fit the form instead of asking why the form exists in the first place.

Over time, paperwork becomes the language of the system. Questions turn into reference numbers. Delays turn into “processing.” Responsibility dissolves into departments, policies, and procedures that don’t belong to any single person.

Most of the time, this feels reasonable. Necessary, even. Records matter. Consistency matters. But paperwork also does something else: it absorbs friction. It slows change without saying no. It turns power into process.

When everything is routed through forms, it becomes harder to tell where decisions actually live.
