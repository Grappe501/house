Delegation

Responsibility can move without moving outcomes.

One of the most reliable features of a system is its ability to move responsibility.

A question is raised and redirected. A complaint is acknowledged and routed. A request is received and assigned a number.

Each step creates the feeling of motion. Someone is now responsible. Someone is now handling it.

Often, that is true.

But delegation does not automatically change what the system is built to produce. It changes who must carry the work of explanation, response, and containment.

This is why outcomes can remain stable even as names change. The work moves. The structure remains.

Delegation can be useful. It distributes load. It prevents one point from breaking under constant demand.

It can also function as insulation. The closer you get to the source of consequence, the more likely you are to meet a layer designed to absorb impact before it reaches the core.

This is not deception. It is design.

Systems that cannot delegate collapse quickly. Systems that delegate well can preserve their shape even under strain.

So a shift in responsibility is not, by itself, evidence of change. It may be evidence that the system is regulating pressure in the way it was built to.

Reconstruction requires noticing the difference: between movement that redistributes burden, and movement that alters the mechanism producing the outcome.
