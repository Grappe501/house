Rhythm

After enough repetitions, the body stops asking questions.

Not because the questions were answered.

Because the pattern became reliable.

You learn the timing of it—how long the wait usually takes, where the bottleneck always is, which step will require a second attempt. You start preparing for friction before it arrives, the way you prepare for weather.

The rhythm is subtle at first.

Log in.
Catch up.
Respond.
Move on.

Even when the steps don’t feel meaningful, they feel familiar. Familiarity carries its own kind of comfort. It reduces decision-making. It smooths the day. It makes the system feel less like something happening to you and more like something you’re participating in.

But rhythm can also do something dangerous: it turns adaptation into identity.

You become the kind of person who can keep pace. Who doesn’t fall behind. Who knows how to recover lost ground quickly. And the cost of that competence is that the tempo becomes unquestioned.

If the rhythm is always running you, the first act of agency is noticing the beat.
