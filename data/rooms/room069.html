Act III — Reconstruction (REBUILD) • Room 069

Interfaces

Change travels through handoffs.

Most structures do not fail at their strongest points.

They fail at the places where one part hands work to another.

Interfaces are the seams of a system. Where responsibility changes hands. Where information is translated. Where intent becomes procedure.

On paper, these handoffs look clean. Inputs go in. Outputs come out. Each part does its job.

In practice, interfaces carry friction. Context is lost. Timing slips. Small assumptions compound into large gaps.

Change must pass through these seams. It does not leap over them.

This is why plans that look sound in isolation can dissolve when they move. Each handoff requires translation. Each translation introduces loss.

Systems that endure are not the ones with flawless components. They are the ones with interfaces designed to tolerate misunderstanding, delay, and variation without breaking.

When an interface is weak, pressure accumulates upstream. People compensate. Workarounds appear. Informal fixes take root to keep things moving.

From a distance, this looks like resilience. Up close, it is often a sign that the joint cannot carry the load assigned to it.

Improving an interface does not require changing intentions. It requires reducing friction. Clarifying handoffs. Making failure visible earlier, when it is easier to correct.

Reconstruction becomes practical when attention shifts from grand redesigns to the quiet places where change is lost in transit.

Strength at the seams allows the rest of the structure to matter. Without it, even well-sequenced work arrives fragmented— and the system absorbs the effort without changing shape.
